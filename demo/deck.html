The problem

More data is not automatically better data

EgoVerse is a library of videos of people doing everyday tasks, filmed from a camera strapped to their head. Robots learn how to move by watching them.

It holds hundreds of thousands of clips. Some are broken. Many are near-copies of each other. Training on all of it is slow, and the copies teach the robot nothing new.

So: which ones are actually worth training on? Nobody can answer that today without guessing. That is the question we took.

Why this question

It's the one we could actually check our answer to

We chose this

Which videos are worth training on?

Option two

How varied is this set of videos?

Option three

Did the person in the video succeed?

Option three needs someone to watch hundreds of videos and mark which ones went well. That list doesn't exist, so we'd have had to make it ourselves — and then grade our own homework against it.

Our question avoids that. Train a model on two different sets of videos, then see which model does better on videos neither of them has seen. Nobody has to take our word for it.

Before we could start

Most of the library can't answer this question

To prove a set of videos is better, you have to test the model on people and rooms it never trained on. That means you need a collection where several different people filmed in several different rooms, and where somebody wrote down who and where.

Step 1 of 3

Throw out the broken ones

Eight checks, all simple arithmetic on the recorded hand and head positions. No AI deciding anything. Every video we drop records which check caught it.

Something is broken

The hand position data is missing, or frozen, or jumps faster than 10 metres per second — a hand folding laundry moves under 2. That's the tracking glitching, not the person.

It isn't one demonstration

The clip is three seconds long, or the hands barely moved, or it runs three times longer than a normal clip from the same lab — someone left the camera running across a whole laundry session.

In the collection we test on, this drops 49 of 572 videos. In the biggest collection it drops 1 in 7.

Step 1 · what that looks like

A good clip and a bad one

The top two are keepers — the person works in front of themselves, hands in the middle of the picture. The bottom two work off to one side, hands half out of shot for the entire clip. Same task, much worse view of it.

Step 1 · a mistake we made

We got a check wrong, and pictures are what told us

One check was meant to catch clips where the hands leave the picture entirely. It flagged 23 videos. Then we printed those frames out to put in this deck — and the hands were right there in every one.

We tried three different ways to work out whether a hand is in shot. All three disagreed with what the pictures plainly showed. This camera is a fisheye lens looking down at an odd angle, and none of the standard methods cope with it.

So we stopped claiming the hands were invisible, and now claim only what we can prove: the hands were held far off to the side. That is measurable, and you can check it against the frames on the previous slide.

Worth saying: the numbers looked completely reasonable. Nothing about them flagged itself as wrong. We only caught it because we looked at the pictures.

Step 2 of 3

Describe each video as 41 numbers

To pick a varied set, you first need a way of saying whether two videos are similar. We build one from how the person moved — no video, no AI.

Speed

how far the hands travelled, how fast, how jerkily, how much of the clip they were moving at all

Space

how big an area each hand swept out, and where that area sat

Both hands

whether the hands moved together or took turns, and how close they came

Grip & head

how wide the fingers spread, and how much the person turned their head

Two videos are "similar" when these 41 numbers are close. That's all "similar" means here.

Step 3 of 3 · the idea

Four ways to spend the same budget

Same dots, same number of picks. Picking at random spends 18 of 26 inside the big crowded blob. The method we ended up using spends 12 there and reaches all four outlying groups.

The three methods differ in what they ask. "Is everything near something I picked?" · "What's furthest from everything I've picked so far?" · "Which set is most spread out overall?" The last one won.

Step 3 · on the real videos

The same idea, 572 real clips

Both sides keep exactly the same number of videos. The left one ends up with 97 different combinations of person-and-room. The right one gets 20.

That gap is the whole experiment: does spreading your pick out actually buy you anything?

How we score it

What "better" means here

Show the model a third of a second of where someone's hands have been. Ask it where they go next. Compare its guess to what the person actually did. The red shading is how wrong it was. Less red is better.

We do that on clips the model has never seen, for every set of videos we're comparing.

How we score it · being fair

Two things that keep this honest

We didn't invent the score

It's the same one the people who built EgoVerse use in their own paper, and they say plainly that it's a stand-in rather than a measure of whether a robot succeeds. We use it on exactly those terms.

Nothing else changes

Same model, same settings, same test clips every time. Only the training videos differ, so any difference in score has to come from the videos. And the model has no randomness in it, so re-running gives the same answer.

We also ask the model to predict movement relative to where the hand is now — otherwise it could score well just by memorising which room it's in.

How we score it · the comparison

40 head-to-head races

Each time we run this, we pick a different set of people and rooms to hold back for testing. That shifts every score at once, sometimes by more than the effect we're looking for — so averaging the scores together would bury the answer in noise.

Instead we never compare averages. Every method races random on the same test, with the same budget, on the same held-back clips, and we count who wins.

Repeats10

each with a different set held back

Budgets2

a quarter of the videos, and a half

Tests2

new people, and new rooms

10 × 2 × 2 = 40 races per method.

The part to be suspicious of

Why believe a 3.5% difference?

It's a small number. The obvious worry is that our test is too noisy to tell anything apart, and we got lucky 40 times.

So we added a method designed to lose: same number of videos, but taken almost entirely from a handful of people in a handful of rooms.

The deliberately narrow pick1 / 40

races won, and 5.8% worse on average

Why that settles it

The EgoVerse team already showed that variety in people and rooms helps. Our test finds the same thing. So it can tell a good pick from a bad one — which means the smaller numbers above are real, not noise.

Where it stops

Using everything is still best

Training on all the videos beats every quarter and half we picked, including our best one. Picking well does not beat having more.

What we're not saying

"Delete 75% of your data for free."

What we actually measured

At a quarter of the data, a varied pick closes about 45% of the gap between a random quarter and using everything.

That second number is the useful one if you're deciding what to collect next, what to pay someone to label, or what to fit on a robot.

The broken-clip check, measured

Long clips aren't better data — just more of it

In the biggest collection, 1 in 7 clips runs more than three times the normal length. Someone left the camera going across a whole laundry session and saved it as one clip. Are those worth keeping?

If both sides get the same…Dropping themResult
number of clipswins 1 of 101.7% worse

Count clips and dropping them looks like a mistake. But one long clip holds sixty times the footage of a normal one — so the side that kept them was quietly training on twice as much material.

Give both sides the same amount of footage and dropping them wins every time. The long clips were never better data. There was just more of it.

Did we fiddle with it?

The answer doesn't depend on our settings

Fair thing to ask. So we re-ran the whole comparison nine times with different settings for the scoring model — bigger, smaller, more memory, less memory, looking further ahead and less far ahead. We didn't re-tune anything to suit any particular method.

Things we learned along the way

Three things about this library worth knowing

A trap in the files

The data files pad every clip out with blank frames at the end. Read them without trimming and every clip looks like the tracking froze. Our check would have thrown away the entire library.

Video is expensive

The video for these clips is 84.6 GB. The recorded hand positions are 2.3 GB. We built everything on the positions on purpose — a filter that costs more to run than the training it saves isn't worth having.

A "clip" isn't one thing

A clip is 90 seconds in one lab and 11 in another. If you ask for "1,000 clips" from two labs you get wildly different amounts of footage. Ask for hours, not clips.

Both labs came back completely clean on missing and frozen tracking — 0% each. That's worth knowing too.

Limitations

What we'd attack if this were someone else's

  • Our score isn't robot success. It measures how well a model predicts human hand movement. It does not tell you a robot trained on the winner folds more shirts.
  • The scoring model never sees the video. That falls out of the 84.6 GB problem. A model that looked at pictures might rank these sets differently, and we couldn't tell.
  • One task, one lab. We can't promise this carries over. And we didn't choose that lab because it suited us — it was the only one with the right labels.
  • Our first-choice method lost. We went in expecting one approach to win and a different one did. We left the loser in the table rather than quietly reordering the story.

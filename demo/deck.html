The brief

Three questions nobody can answer deterministically

Track 1 — ours

Which episodes are worth training on?

Track 2

How diverse is this subset, really?

Track 3

Did the human actually succeed?

We took Track 1 for one reason: it is the only one of the three whose answer can be validated without inventing ground truth. Track 3 would need labels that do not exist in EgoVerse — we would have had to hand-label them ourselves and then grade our own homework.

The claim

Stated so that it can fail

At a fixed episode budget K, a quality-gated, coverage-maximizing subset trains a better policy than K episodes sampled uniformly at random.

Everything in the repo exists to test that one sentence. The budget is held fixed, so this is never "more data wins" — it is the same amount of data, chosen differently.

Note what the claim does not say: nothing about robot success rates, nothing about other tasks, nothing about other labs.

Generalization has to be measured against something held out. We hold out unseen operators and unseen scenes — the EgoVerse paper's own two axes. That needs a populated operator×scene grid, and rl2 is the only slice that has one (190 of 320 cells filled).

The biggest slice, microagi at 9,896 episodes, is unusable here — it carries no operator or scene labels at all.

Method

Four steps, all deterministic

01
Quality gate
Tracking dropout, frozen tracker, hands out of frame, degenerate duration, near-zero motion. Every drop carries a reason string you can audit. 29 of 572 dropped.
02
Episode embedding
41 trajectory and hand-keypoint features per episode — path length, speed moments, workspace volume, bimanual coordination, grasp aperture, head motion. Whitened.
03
Selection
Greedy facility location (monotone submodular → 1−1/e guarantee), plus DPP log-det and k-center as ablations, against random as the baseline.
04
Validation
Train an identical policy on each subset. Compare offline Avg-MSE on held-out unseen operators and unseen scenes.

No LLM-as-judge anywhere. Every scoring component is inspectable.

The metric

We borrowed the authors' own proxy — deliberately

“we report primarily the offline Avg-MSE metric… While this metric does not directly measure downstream robot performance, it provides a stable signal for comparing generalization.”

— EgoVerse, arXiv 2604.07607

This matters for defensibility. When someone objects that Avg-MSE is not robot success, the answer is not a defence of our judgement — the dataset's own authors already conceded the point and used it anyway, for exactly the purpose we use it: ranking training subsets against each other.

Our policy: 10 frames of proprioceptive history → 30-step future bimanual end-effector chunk, predicted relative to the current pose so it cannot win by memorising which room it is in. Closed-form ridge, so the fit is exact and seed-independent — any difference between conditions comes from the data, not from optimiser noise.

Experiment design

Why every comparison is paired

Each seed draws a different held-out split, which shifts absolute Avg-MSE for every condition at once. Across-seed error bars would drown a real effect in split variance.

So each selector is compared to random at the same seed, the same budget, on the same held-out windows.

Selection matters here; filtering does not. The quality gate is statistically indistinguishable from no effect. It drops only 5% of rl2, almost all for hands leaving frame — on data this clean there is nothing for filtering to bite on.

We report that because it reverses what we concluded at 3 seeds, where the gate looked worth about a percent.

The slide that matters

Why you should believe a 4% margin

A few percent on a proxy metric, on a few hundred episodes, is easy to disbelieve. The obvious failure mode is a harness too noisy to detect anything, where our margin is luck.

So we added a condition that should lose, for a reason established independently by the EgoVerse paper: demonstrator and scene diversity improve generalization.

degenerate spends the identical budget concentrated into as few operator×scene groups as possible. It loses 1/40 at +7.17%. The harness detects the effect the paper reported — so the smaller margins are measurements, not noise.

Honesty

What we are not claiming

More data still beats better data at this scale. The full gated pool beats every 25% and 50% subset, including ours.

The overclaim we avoided

“Throw away 75% of your data for free.”

What we actually measured

At ¼ budget, diversity selection closes 56% (unseen operator) and 50% (unseen scene) of the gap between a random quarter and using everything.

That second version is the one useful to somebody deciding what to label, transfer, or train on next — and it is the one we can defend.

Robustness

Did we tune the proxy to get this?

The obvious attack. So we re-ran the comparison across a grid of proxy configurations — regularisation (0.1 / 1 / 10), feature width (256 / 512 / 1024), history (5 / 10 / 20 frames), horizon (15 / 30 / 60 steps) — re-tuning nothing per condition.

dpp beats random in9/9

configurations · mean −6.52% · worst −5.70%

degenerate beats random in0/9

configurations · mean +11.81%

The ranking is unchanged in every configuration tested, and the control loses in every one. If the conclusion only held at one setting, it would not be a conclusion.

Along the way

Three things we found in the data

Trap

Zarr arrays are zero-padded to a chunk boundary. Read them raw and the trailing frames look exactly like a frozen tracker. The gate would have discarded the entire dataset. True length is total_frames in the group attrs.

Cost

Images are 84.6 GB for this slice; poses are 2.3 GB. Every signal we use is pose-only, deliberately — a curation engine that costs more than the training it saves is not worth running.

Comparability

An “episode” is not a common unit. Median 93 s in rl2 vs 6.6 s in mecka — a factor of 14. Episode-count budgets do not transfer across labs; budget in seconds.

Both labs are clean on tracking dropout and frozen trackers — 0.0% each. That is a real finding about EgoVerse, not a null result.

Limitations

The things to attack first

  • Avg-MSE is a proxy, not robot success. It ranks subsets; it does not predict success rate. The EgoVerse authors say the same and use it anyway.
  • The proxy policy is proprioceptive, not visual — a direct consequence of the 84.6 GB figure. A visual policy might rank subsets differently.
  • One task, one lab. No claim of transfer. The slice was forced by metadata availability, not chosen because it flattered us.
  • Facility location was our a priori pick and it lost to DPP and k-center. Reported, not quietly reordered so the winner looks like the plan.
  • Small effect, small pool. ~4% on a few hundred episodes. The positive control is what makes that interpretable rather than decorative.

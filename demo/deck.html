Picking a track

Why we took Track 1

Track 1 — ours

Which episodes are worth training on?

Track 2

How diverse is this subset, really?

Track 3

Did the human actually succeed?

Track 3 needs labels saying which demos succeeded. EgoVerse has none. We would have had to label a few hundred episodes ourselves and then score our classifier against our own labels, with no second annotator to check us.

Track 1 lets us skip that. We can train a model on two different subsets and measure which one does better on data neither of them saw. Nobody has to take our word for it.

The question

One sentence, written so it could come out false

At a fixed budget of K episodes, a quality-gated, coverage-maximizing subset trains a better policy than K episodes picked at random.

The budget is fixed on both sides. This is never "more data wins" — it's the same amount of data, chosen two different ways.

Things the sentence does not claim: anything about robot success rates, anything about other tasks, anything about other labs. We'll come back to all three at the end.

Before any modelling

Most of EgoVerse can't answer this question

To show a subset generalizes you need to hold something out. We hold out operators we never trained on and scenes we never trained on. That needs a lab where several people each recorded in several rooms. Only one lab has that.

Step 1 of 4

The gate: throw out episodes that are broken

Six rules, all arithmetic on the pose arrays. No model, no judgement call. Every dropped episode records which rule caught it. Two of the rules scale to the lab's own median episode length, because a "normal" episode is 93 s in one lab and 11 s in another.

rl2 is essentially spotless: 1 episode in 572. The biggest slice of fold_clothes data, microagi, drops 1 in 7.

A note on the thresholds: we started with a 99th-percentile rule and threw it out. A percentile drops exactly 1% of whatever you point it at, clean or filthy, so it can't tell you anything. A multiple of the median can come back 0% or 14%, and here it does both.

Step 1 · a rule we deleted

We had a seventh rule. We couldn't prove it, so we cut it.

It projected the hand keypoints into the camera image and flagged episodes where the hands left the frame. It dropped 23 episodes. Then we rendered those frames to put in this deck, and the hands were plainly visible in every one.

Attempt 1

Treat keypoints as camera-frame points and use the stored 3×4 K matrix. 0 of 42 keypoints land in frame.

Attempt 2

Transform by the head pose first, then project. 0 of 42 keypoints land in frame.

What we did

Deleted the rule. We could not establish the stored coordinate convention, so every number it produced was unfounded.

The remaining six rules use durations, distances and NaN counts only. None of them touches camera geometry, which is also why they compare cleanly across labs.

Worth saying out loud: the histogram of that signal looked perfectly reasonable. Nothing about it flagged itself as wrong. Rendering the frames is what caught it, and we only did that because we wanted a picture for a slide.

Step 2 of 4

Describe each episode as 41 numbers

To pick a spread-out subset you need a notion of which episodes resemble each other. We build one from the hand and head trajectories. No video, no neural network.

Motion

path length, mean and peak speed, acceleration spread, how much of the episode the hand was actually moving

Workspace

the bounding box each hand swept, its volume, where its centre sat

Two hands

correlation between the two hands' speeds, distance between them, closest approach

Grip & view

finger spread around the wrist over time, how much the head moved and turned

Then whiten every column so no single feature dominates the distances. Two episodes are "similar" if these 41 numbers are close.

Step 3 of 4 · what the selectors do

Four ways to spend the same budget

Same 346 points, same 26 picks. Random spends 18 of them inside the big blob. Log-det spends 12 and reaches all four pockets.

Facility location asks: is every episode close to something I picked? k-center asks: what's the furthest thing from everything I've picked so far? Log-det (DPP) asks: which set spans the most volume, so no two picks are redundant?

Step 4 of 4 · the measurement

What Avg-MSE actually is

Train a small model on the subset. Show it 10 frames of where the hands have been, ask it where they go for the next 30 frames. Subtract its answer from what the human did, square it, average over every number, every window, every held-out episode. Lower is better.

Step 4 · why this metric

It's the authors' metric, not ours

“we report primarily the offline Avg-MSE metric… While this metric does not directly measure downstream robot performance, it provides a stable signal for comparing generalization.”

— EgoVerse, arXiv 2604.07607

We chose it because when someone objects that Avg-MSE isn't robot success, we don't have to defend our own taste. The people who built the dataset already made this trade and wrote down why.

Two details that keep it honest. We predict hand motion relative to where the hand is now, so the model can't score points by memorising which room it's in. And the model is closed-form ridge regression, so there's no training noise. Same subset in, same numbers out, every time.

Experiment design

Every comparison is head-to-head

Each seed picks a different set of held-out operators and scenes. That shifts the score for every method at once, sometimes by more than the effect we're looking for. Averaging across seeds and quoting a standard deviation would bury the result in that noise.

So we never compare averages. Each method is compared to random on the same seed, the same budget, and the same held-out episodes, and we count how often it wins.

Choosing well matters. Filtering doesn't — here. Skipping the gate costs nothing measurable on rl2, which makes sense: it only drops 1 episode in 572. On microagi, where it drops 1 in 7, it would presumably matter a great deal. We can't test that, because microagi has no operator or scene labels to hold out.

This also reverses what we said after 3 seeds, when the gate looked worth about a percent. Ten seeds killed it.

The part to be suspicious of

Why believe a 4% difference?

It's a small number, on a proxy metric, over a few hundred episodes. The obvious worry is that our measurement is too noisy to see anything and we got lucky.

So we added a method designed to lose. Same budget, but spent inside as few operator×scene groups as possible: about 21 instead of about 73.

Degenerate1 / 40

+7.01% Avg-MSE, p = 1e−11

Why that settles it

The EgoVerse paper already showed that operator and scene variety improve generalization. Our setup reproduces that. So it can detect a real effect of this kind, and the smaller numbers above aren't noise.

The control also gets weaker at the 50% budget, which is what should happen — with half the pool you can't concentrate the picks very much. A control that stayed flat would have meant we'd built it wrong.

Where it stops

More data still beats better data

Training on all 565 gated episodes beats every 25% and 50% subset we built, including the winning one.

Training setUnseen operatorUnseen scene

So the claim isn't "delete 75% of your data for free." It's this: at a quarter of the budget, choosing well recovers 52% of the distance between a random quarter and using everything.

That's the number worth having if you're deciding what to label next, what to ship to a robot, or what to pay someone to collect.

Things we learned about the dataset

Three findings that cost us time

Padding

Zarr arrays are zero-padded out to a chunk boundary. Read one straight and the tail looks exactly like a frozen tracker. Our gate would have deleted the entire dataset. The real length is total_frames in the group attributes.

Cost

Video for this slice is 84.6 GB. The pose arrays are 2.3 GB. We built everything on poses on purpose: a curation step that costs more than the training run it saves isn't worth running.

Units

An "episode" means different things in different labs. Median 93 s in rl2, 6.6 s in mecka. If you budget in episode counts across labs you're comparing 14:1. Budget in seconds.

Both labs came back clean on tracking dropout and frozen trackers, 0.0% each. That's worth knowing, not a null result.

Limitations

What we'd attack if this were someone else's

  • Avg-MSE isn't robot success. It ranks subsets. It doesn't tell you a robot trained on the winner will pick up more shirts.
  • The scoring model never sees the video. That falls out of the 84.6 GB number. A vision-based policy could rank these subsets differently and we couldn't tell.
  • One task, one lab. We can't claim this transfers. We didn't choose rl2 because it suited us, we chose it because it was the only slice with the metadata.
  • We can't price the gate where it matters. It drops 1 in 7 microagi episodes and 1 in 572 rl2 episodes, but only rl2 has the operator and scene labels our experiment needs. So the place the gate does something is the place we can't measure it.
  • Facility location was our pick going in, and it lost to log-det and k-center. We left it in the table rather than quietly reordering the story.
